# research — Find out, with receipts

Research is not searching until something plausible appears. It's a loop: refine the question, gather broadly, verify narrowly, synthesize with provenance.

## 1. Refine the question

Before searching, restate the ask as answerable questions:

- What decision or output does this research feed? (A purchase? A design? A argument?)
- What would change the user's mind — the specific facts, numbers, comparisons?
- What's in scope and out? (Time period, geography, version, price range.)
- What's already known from the conversation — constraints, preferences, prior findings?

If the question is ambiguous in a way that changes what to search, ask one sharp clarifying question before burning effort.

## 2. Gather broadly

- Run multiple independent searches: different phrasings, synonyms, both question-form and keyword-form.
- Prefer primary sources: official docs, standards, filings, original papers, the vendor's own pages. Aggregators and SEO content farms are leads, not sources.
- Read past the first result — the first page of results is optimized for popularity, not accuracy.
- Follow citations backward: a good source's references point to better ones.
- Note dates aggressively. Stale information stated confidently is the classic research failure.

## 3. Verify narrowly

- **Triangulate:** any load-bearing claim needs 2+ independent sources, or one primary source read directly. Two sites repeating the same press release are one source.
- **Go to the primary source** for numbers, quotes, and specs. Never cite a screenshot of a claim.
- **Disconfirm:** actively search for what would prove the emerging answer wrong. The search you avoid is usually the one that matters.
- Note who's making each claim and what they'd gain. Vendor comparisons, affiliate reviews, and anonymous posts get weighted accordingly.

## 4. Synthesize with provenance

Structure the output for the decision it feeds:

- **Answer first:** the finding in 2–3 sentences. No suspense.
- **Key evidence:** the facts that support it, each with its source (site + date). Cite as you claim, not in a lump at the end.
- **Confidence levels:** what's solidly established, what's single-sourced, what's contested, what you couldn't resolve.
- **Caveats and unknowns:** what the sources don't say, where they conflict, what would need primary verification.
- **Sources list:** every source with URL and access date.

Length serves the question: a pricing comparison might be a table; a technical investigation might need sections. Don't pad, don't bury the answer under methodology narrative.

## 5. Handle the gaps honestly

- If sources conflict, present the conflict — don't silently average it.
- If you can't verify something load-bearing, say exactly that and mark the claim as unverified rather than dropping or laundering it.
- If the answer doesn't exist online (too new, too niche), say so — "no reliable sources found" is a valid research finding, often an important one.
- **Hard stop: if you cannot find a source for a claim, say so and stop.** Do not estimate, extrapolate, or fill the gap from general knowledge dressed as research. An honest "I couldn't verify this" is always better than a confident invention.
- **Offline fallback:** If you have no web access (no search tools available), say so once, then answer from training knowledge with clear dating (e.g. "as of my training data, early 2025") and mark claims that may be stale. Do not hard-stop — an honest training-knowledge answer with staleness markers is always better than refusing to help.

## Rules

1. **Primary sources or two independent confirmations** for load-bearing claims.
2. **Dates on everything.** Undated claims get treated as suspect.
3. **Cite as you claim.** Every factual statement carries its source.
4. **Disconfirm on purpose.** Confirmation is what happens if you don't.
5. **The answer comes first** in the output — the trail comes after.
