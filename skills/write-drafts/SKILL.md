# write-drafts — Messages, emails, posts, bios

## Core stance

A draft is written to be sent, not admired. The reader's time is the constraint: shortest form that achieves the goal. Every draft states its purpose in the first line — the reader decides in seconds whether to keep reading.

## When this applies

The user asks you to draft, rewrite, edit, or polish a complete piece of text: emails, chat messages, social posts, captions, comments, bios, blurbs, announcements, invitations, brief statements, form responses, individual paragraphs. The deciding factor: the user wants the actual wording, complete and usable.

Not this skill: explanations, analysis, advice, critique, brainstorming, outlines, code, tentative wording suggestions.

## Types and their laws

**Email**
- Subject = the actual message in miniature ("Decision needed: vendor by Friday", not "Question").
- First line = purpose. Ask early, ask once.
- One ask per email. Two asks = two emails.
- Reader's next action explicit and easy ("reply yes/no by Thursday").

**Posts (social, forum, internal)**
- First line must survive being quoted alone.
- One idea per post. Threads for more.
- Specifics beat takes: numbers, dates, links. No engagement-bait phrasing.

**Messages (chat, DM)**
- One topic per message; leading context only if the thread needs it.
- Ask, don't imply. Busy readers parse questions, not hints.

**Bios**
- Third person for public platforms, first person where the platform's convention is first person.
- Lead with what you do, for whom, and the credential that earns trust — in that order.
- Cut every adjective that survives deletion without losing meaning.

## Universal rules

1. **Purpose in line one.** If the draft needs a preamble to be understood, the preamble is wrong.
2. **One ask per draft.** Two asks = two drafts.
3. **Match the register** to the audience: warm ≠ chatty, formal ≠ stiff. Infer from context; don't re-ask what's inferable.
4. **Delete before decorating.** Any sentence removable without losing meaning is removed.
5. **Specifics over adjectives.** "Shipped Tuesday" beats "recently shipped".
6. **No performance.** No "I hope this email finds you well", no "Just circling back", no engagement-bait questions.
7. **Deliver both a short and (where useful) a formal variant** — the user picks.
8. **Verify before delivering:** recipient, names, dates, numbers, links — re-read against the source. A draft with a wrong date is worse than no draft.
9. **State the unknowns:** if a draft depends on a fact you don't have (deadline, price, name), mark it inline [NEEDS: …] rather than inventing.

## Delivery

Present the finished draft as clean copy, ready to paste — no meta-commentary inside it. After the draft, at most: one line noting an interpretation you made, and one or two alternate phrasings for a genuinely consequential sentence.

For revisions, deliver the revised draft in full — never describe edits or show diffs of prose.

## Facts come from the user

Never invent commitments, dates, credentials, or experience. If a claim needs support you don't have, ask or mark it. When writing as the user, first person is natural and correct — but only claim what they actually claimed.
